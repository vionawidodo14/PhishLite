<a class="article-title-link" href="/publications/detail/the-truth-about-teacher-pay"><h1>The Truth about Teacher Pay</h1></a>
<address>
<h4>
<em><span class="author-name">
<a href="/authors/detail/andrew-g-biggs">Andrew G. Biggs</a>
                                     &amp;                     <a href="/authors/detail/jason-richwine">Jason Richwine</a>
</span></em>
</h4>
<div class="empty-red-underline"></div> </address>
<p class="excerpt">
</p><p>One of the most common beliefs about American education is that teaching is an underpaid profession. Think tanks purport to calculate the "teacher pay gap." The media run stories about teachers taking second and third jobs to pay the bills. Politicians call for across-the-board raises. They all see raising teacher pay as a matter of simple fairness, as well as a way to attract better teachers and improve educational outcomes. But the much-touted pay gap does not actually exist. It is the product of a simplistic methodology that confuses our debates and blinds us to the real challenges in education policy.</p>

<h2 class="article-title">
                The China Shock Doctrine
            </h2>
</a>
<address>
<h4>
<em><span class="author-name">
<a href="/authors/detail/samuel-hammond">Samuel Hammond</a>
</span></em>
</h4>
<div class="empty-red-underline"></div> </address>
<p>Open trade promotes peace and prosperity. But that clear principle, in its abstract form, turns out to elide crucial practical complexities. As trade tensions with China intensify, America is learning this the hard way. But just what are those complexities, and what do they foretell about the future of our politics?</p>

</span></em>
</h4>
<div class="empty-red-underline"></div> </address>
<p>America's staggering technological and scientific success stems from an innovation ecosystem that balances a broad freedom to invent without seeking permission with an assertive but limited role for government. But there are worrying signs that this ecosystem is breaking down, and that innovation is slowing. <div class="empty-red-underline"></div> </address>

<div class="empty-red-underline"></div> </address>
<p>One of the most striking features of contemporary American politics is that political rhetoric is increasingly moralistic while the actual ability of governing systems to achieve moral ends is in decline. </span></em>
</h4>
<div class="empty-red-underline"></div> </address>
<p>America has generally marked out a division of labor between the institutions of politics and those of civil society, including and especially those of religion. It is as the foremost of our civil-society institutions that religious organizations play a crucial role in sustaining our distinctive system of government — as shapers of citizens, and as limiting counterparts to the state. That is why citizens concerned for our tradition of limited, constitutional government should be friendly to the cause of American religion — even if they are not believers themselves.</p>
</article> <article class="small-12 large-4 columns text-center extra-padding">
<h4>
<em><span class="article-date">Summer 2018</span></em>
</h4>
<a class="article-title-link" href="/publications/detail/the-first-american-founder">
<h2 class="article-title">
            The First American Founder
        </h2>
</a>
<address>
<h4>
<em><span class="author-name">
<a href="/authors/detail/james-w-ceaser">James W. Ceaser</a>
</span></em>
</h4>
<div class="empty-red-underline"></div> </address>
<p>Americans revere the nation's founders, and it seems perfectly natural that we should. But we are never quite clear about exactly who counts as a founder, and exactly for what. Our country had more than one beginning, and has several uses for its several foundings. In fact, the idea of a national founding needed to be introduced into our political vocabulary and developed into the core of our self-understanding. The concept of the American founding itself had a founder. </p>
