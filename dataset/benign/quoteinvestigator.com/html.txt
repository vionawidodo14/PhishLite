<h2 class="entry-title"><a href="https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/10/25/humble/" rel="bookmark">Don’t Be So Humble—You’re Not That Great</a></h2> </header><!-- .entry-header -->
<div class="entry-content">
<p><strong>Golda Meir? Simcha Dinitz? Apocryphal?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Dear Quote Investigator:</strong> A humble brag is a statement that on its surface appears to be modest or self-deprecating; however, the true intent is to highlight a success or achievement. The funniest response I have seen to these types of comments is the following:</p>
<blockquote><p>Don’t act so humble; you aren’t that great.</p></blockquote>
<p>Would you please explore the provenance of this remark?</p>
<p><strong>Quote Investigator:</strong> This statement was circulating many years before the term humble brag was coined. “The New York Times” published a piece in 1969 about Golda Meir who had recently become the Prime Minister of Israel. The article was based on the memories of Simcha Dinitz who worked as a close aide to Meir from 1963 to 1966. Boldface added to excerpts by <strong>QI</strong>: <a class="simple-footnote" href="#note-436721-1" id="return-note-436721-1" title="1969 March 18, New York Times, A Tangy Flavor in Mrs. Meir’s Views (Special to The New York Times), Quote Page 12, Column 4, New York. (ProQuest)"><sup>1</sup></a></p>
<blockquote><p>“There is nobody like Golda for seeing what needs doing—or saying,” Mr. Dinitz commented. “She is always telling people: <strong>‘Don’t be so humble—you’re not that great.’</strong>”</p></blockquote>
<p>Below are additional selected citations in chronological order.</p>

<h2 class="entry-title"><a href="https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/10/23/traffic/" rel="bookmark">It Is Easy To Predict an Automobile in 1880; It Is Very Hard To Predict a Traffic Problem</a></h2> </header><!-- .entry-header -->
<div class="entry-content">
<p><strong>Frederik Pohl? Robert Heinlein? Isaac Asimov? Connie Willis? Ed Bryant? George Zebrowski? Ben Bova? Robert J. Sawyer? Sam Moskowitz?</strong></p>
<p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-436712" height="201" sizes="(max-width: 540px) 100vw, 540px" src="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/trafficj07.jpg" srcset="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/trafficj07.jpg 540w, https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/trafficj07-300x112.jpg 300w" width="540"/><strong>Dear Quote Investigator:</strong> Predicting the primary effects of a new technology is difficult but feasible. Anticipating all the secondary effects is nearly impossible. Here are two statements of a viewpoint that has achieved popularity amongst science fiction aficionados:</p>
<blockquote><p>In the nineteenth century a machine enthusiast could have predicted the automobile, but an SF writer could have predicted the traffic jam.</p>
<p>It is easy to predict the automobile but difficult to predict the traffic jam.</p></blockquote>
<p>Would you please explore this saying?</p>
<p><strong>Quote Investigator:</strong> The earliest match located by <strong>QI</strong> occurred in a 1953 essay by prolific science and SF author Isaac Asimov titled “Social Science Fiction”. Asimov discussed three different types of SF stories: <a class="simple-footnote" href="#note-436711-1" id="return-note-436711-1" title="1977, Turning Points: Essays on the Art of Science Fiction, Edited by Damon Knight, Social Science Fiction by Isaac Asimov, (From Modern Science Fiction, Its Meaning and Its Future, ed. Reginald Bretnor, Coward-McCann, 1953), Start Page 29, Quote Page 40, Harper &amp; Row, New York. (Verified with scans)"><sup>1</sup></a></p>
<blockquote><p>Let us suppose it is 1880 and we have a series of three writers who are each interested in writing a story of the future about an imaginary vehicle that can move without horses by some internal source of power; a horseless carriage, in other words.</p></blockquote>
<p>According to Asimov, gadget SF, the first type of tale, highlights the struggle to invent such a device and climaxes with its successful demonstration. Adventure SF, the second type, presents a romantic tale that hinges on using the device during action packed scenes. Social SF, the third type, explores the complex ramifications of the device as it is deployed within a society.</p>
<p>Asimov remarked that automobiles catalyzed the construction of suburbs. He also observed that vast networks of busy roadways resulted in large numbers of injuries and deaths. These indirect consequences of automobile usage would not have been easy to foresee. Boldface added to excerpts by <strong>QI</strong>: <a class="simple-footnote" href="#note-436711-2" id="return-note-436711-2" title="1977, Turning Points: Essays on the Art of Science Fiction, Edited by Damon Knight, Social Science Fiction by Isaac Asimov, (From Modern Science Fiction, Its Meaning and Its Future, ed. Reginald Bretnor, Coward-McCann, 1953), Start Page 29, Quote Page 41, Harper &amp; Row, New York. (Verified with scans)"><sup>2</sup></a></p>
<blockquote><p><strong>It is easy to predict an automobile in 1880; it is very hard to predict a traffic problem.</strong> The former is really only an extrapolation of the railroad. The latter is something completely novel and unexpected.</p></blockquote>
<p>Below are additional selected citations in chronological order.</p>

<h2 class="entry-title"><a href="https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/10/19/barmaid/" rel="bookmark">An Alleged Scientific Discovery Has No Merit Unless It Can Be Explained To a Barmaid</a></h2> </header><!-- .entry-header -->
<div class="entry-content">
<p><strong>Albert Einstein? Ernest Rutherford? Cyril Hinshelwood? Apocryphal?</strong></p>
<p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-436685" height="252" sizes="(max-width: 308px) 100vw, 308px" src="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/folies08.jpg" srcset="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/folies08.jpg 308w, https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/folies08-300x245.jpg 300w" width="308"/><strong>Dear Quote Investigator:</strong> It should be possible to explain a valid scientific theory to anybody, e.g., a nine-year-old, a grandmother, or the man in the street. This dubious assertion is challenged by the fact that few humans are able to comprehend the notion of a four-dimensional space-time manifold which is central to the breakthrough theory of special relativity in physics.</p>
<p>Would you please explore another debatable claim of this type? Here are three versions:</p>
<ul>
<li>A good scientific theory should be explicable to a barmaid</li>
<li>It should be possible to explain the laws of physics to a barmaid.</li>
<li>No physical theory is worth much if it cannot be explained to a barmaid.</li>
</ul>
<p>This remark has been attributed to both Albert Einstein and Ernest Rutherford, two Nobel Prize winning scientists.</p>
<p><strong>Quote Investigator:</strong> The earliest match located by <strong>QI</strong> appeared in the journal “Biographical Memoirs of Fellows of the Royal Society” within a 1955 article about Albert Einstein who had died earlier in the year. The piece noted that some fellow scientists were initially reluctant to accept Einstein’s research results because of their complex abstract nature. While discussing this resistance the article mentioned the saying together with an ascription to Ernest Rutherford. Boldface added to excerpts by <strong>QI</strong>: <a class="simple-footnote" href="#note-436684-1" id="return-note-436684-1" title="1955 November, Biographical Memoirs of Fellows of the Royal Society, Volume 1, Albert Einstein 1879-1955 by Edmund Whittaker, Start Page 37, Quote Page 54, Published by Royal Society, United Kingdom. (JSTOR) link"><sup>1</sup></a></p>
<blockquote><p>Some of it may have been due to the popular principle attributed to Rutherford, that <strong>an alleged scientific discovery has no merit unless it can be explained to a barmaid.</strong></p></blockquote>
<p>Over time Einstein’s colleagues embraced his work and performed experiments that supported his theories.</p>
<p>Ernest Rutherford died in 1937, so the attribution above is posthumous and rather late. Also, the phrasing has been highly variable. Over all, the supporting evidence is not strong. On the other hand, Rutherford is the leading candidate because other ascriptions only emerged in the 1970s.</p>

<h2 class="entry-title"><a href="https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/10/14/boots/" rel="bookmark">His Grace Returned From the Wars This Morning and Pleasured Me Twice in His Top-Boots</a></h2> </header><!-- .entry-header -->
<div class="entry-content">
<p><strong>Sarah Churchill? James Agate? A. L. Rowse? Theodor Reik? Apocryphal?</strong></p>
<p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-436612" height="220" sizes="(max-width: 369px) 100vw, 369px" src="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/schurchill08.jpg" srcset="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/schurchill08.jpg 369w, https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/schurchill08-300x179.jpg 300w" width="369"/><strong>Dear Quote Investigator:</strong> A legend asserts that Sarah Churchill, Duchess of Marlborough wrote a passionate remark in her diary. Here are three versions:</p>
<ol>
<li>Today the Duke returned from the war and pleasured me twice in his top boots.</li>
<li>My Lord on returning pleasured me thrice without removing his boots.</li>
<li>His Lordship returned from the wars this morning, and pleasured me thrice in his top-boots!</li>
</ol>
<p>Are any of these statements genuine? What evidence is available?</p>
<p><strong>Quote Investigator:</strong> Several researchers have attempted to explore this topic, and the available evidence is weak. Sarah Churchill died in 1744, and the first citation known to <strong>QI</strong> appeared almost two hundred years later in the diaristic autobiography of English theatre critic James Agate. The fourth volume of his autobiography titled “Ego 4” was published in 1940, and it included an entry dated July 28, 1938. Agate discussed his dislike of pageants which included amateur theatrical events. He was unable to suspend his disbelief because he knew the prosaic backgrounds of the performers. Boldface added to excerpts by <strong>QI</strong>: <a class="simple-footnote" href="#note-436609-1" id="return-note-436609-1" title="1940, Ego 4: Yet More of the Autobiography of James Agate by James Agate, Diary Date: July 28, 1938, Quote Page 13, George G. Harrap &amp; Company, London. (Verified with scans)"><sup>1</sup></a></p>
<blockquote><p>How can those be Hengist and Horsa when we know them to be young Mr Pepper and young Mr Salt, the obliging assistants from the local grocer’s ? How can yonder stout party hope to be Sarah, Duchess of Marlborough—<strong>“His Grace returned from the wars this morning and pleasured me twice in his top-boots”</strong>—when we know her to be the vicar’s sister and quite unpleasurable?</p></blockquote>
<p>Agate included the quotation to illustrate the sensuality of Sarah Churchill which the amateur performer was unable to embody and project. Yet, it was unclear how Agate learned of the quotation. Later citations stated that the line was from a family tradition or an oral tradition.</p>
<p>Perhaps there is a closely held diary or letter containing the statement, but <strong>QI</strong> has not yet seen supporting evidence for this hypothesis, and the phrasing has been highly variable.</p>

<h2 class="entry-title"><a href="https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/10/12/ai-shadow/" rel="bookmark">This Is Only a Foretaste of What Is To Come, and Only the Shadow of What Is Going To Be</a></h2> </header><!-- .entry-header -->
<div class="entry-content">
<p><strong>Alan Turing? Apocryphal?</strong></p>
<p><img alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-436603" height="199" sizes="(max-width: 540px) 100vw, 540px" src="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/aint07.jpg" srcset="https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/aint07.jpg 540w, https://quoteinvestigator.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/aint07-300x111.jpg 300w" width="540"/><strong>Dear Quote Investigator:</strong> Code breaker Alan Turing was a major figure in computer science and a pioneer in artificial intelligence. In 2021 Turing’s portrait will appear on newly issued £50 notes from the Bank of England. Would you please explore the quotation that reportedly will be printed on the notes?</p>
<p><strong>Quote Investigator:</strong> In June 1949 “The Times” of London published an article about a Manchester University project which built an electronic calculator referred to hyperbolically as a “mechanical mind”. This early computing device was able to perform a calculation that had heretofore been impossible because of its length and intricacy. Turing’s commentary was both exciting and ominous. Boldface is used to highlight the quotation that will appear on the upcoming bank note: <a class="simple-footnote" href="#note-436601-1" id="return-note-436601-1" title="1949 June 11, The Times, The Mechanical Brain: Answer Found To 300 Year-Old Sum (From Our Special Correspondent), Quote Page 4, Column 5, London, England. (Gale Digital Archive of The Times of London)"><sup>1</sup></a></p>
<blockquote><p>Mr. Turing said yesterday: “<strong>This is only a foretaste of what is to come, and only the shadow of what is going to be.</strong> We have to have some experience with the machine before we really know its capabilities. It may take years before we settle down to the new possibilities, but I do not see why it should not enter any one of the fields normally covered by the human intellect, and eventually compete on equal terms.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Turing also outlined an important future objective of the project:</p>
<blockquote><p>Their research would be directed to finding the degree of intellectual activity of which a machine was capable, and to what extent it could think for itself.</p></blockquote>
<p>This short article ends with a citation, conclusion, image note, and acknowledgement.</p>

<h2 class="entry-title"><a href="https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/10/11/sixty/" rel="bookmark">One Starts To Get Young at the Age of 60 and Then It’s Too Late</a></h2> </header><!-- .entry-header -->
<div class="entry-content">
<p><strong>Pablo Picasso? Jean Cocteau? Derek Prouse?</strong></p>
<p><strong>Dear Quote Investigator:</strong> The proficiency, creativity, and potency of an artist can grow for decades. Yet, painter and sculptor Pablo Picasso apparently said the following about his change in mentality as he became older. Here are two versions:</p>
<ul>
<li>One starts to get young at 60 and then it is too late.</li>
<li>One starts to get young at the age of sixty, and then it’s too late.</li>
</ul>
<p>Would you please help me to find a citation?</p>
<p><strong>Quote Investigator:</strong> Derek Prouse interviewed the prominent French literary figure and film maker Jean Cocteau shortly before the artist died, and the conversation appeared in “The Sunday Times” of London in October 1963. Cocteau repeated a remark he had heard recently from Pablo Picasso. Boldface added to excerpts by <strong>QI</strong>: <a class="simple-footnote" href="#note-436592-1" id="return-note-436592-1" title="1963 October 27, The Boston Sunday Globe, Cocteau’s Last Observations: One Is Getting Young At 60 … It’s Too Late by Derek Prouse, Quote Page 6A, Column 1 and 2, Boston, Massachusetts. (The interview originally appeared in “The Sunday Times” of London on October 20, 1963) (Newspapers_com)"><sup>1</sup></a></p>
<blockquote><p>“Of course, the artist’s life has always been a struggle. Picasso said to me the other day: <strong>‘One starts to get young at the age of 60—and then it’s too late.’</strong> Only then does one start to feel free; only then has one learned to strip oneself down to one’s essential creative simplicity.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Thus, the evidence for this quotation is indirect. Cocteau reported the words he ascribed to Picasso during an interview published in “The Sunday Times”.</p>
