<a href="https://venturehacks.com/get-rich" rel="bookmark" title="Permanent Link: How to get rich">
                            How to get rich                            </a>
</h2>

<h4>
<span class="postAuthor"><a href="/about">Nivi</a></span>

<p><iframe allowfullscreen="allowfullscreen" frameborder="0" height="253" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/jemPACNo1_I" width="450"></iframe></p>
<p>For the last few months, Naval and I have been publishing a podcast on “<a href="https://startupboy.com/2019/02/28/seek-wealth/">How to Get Rich</a>.” It’s based on his chart-busting <a href="https://twitter.com/naval/status/1002103360646823936">tweetstorm</a>. I’ve embedded the first episode above.</p>
<p>You can find it on <a href="https://itunes.apple.com/us/podcast/naval/id1454097755?mt=2">Apple</a>, <a href="https://open.spotify.com/show/7qZAVw03FuurfYnWIWwkHY?si=3B5E_4acTsK-Lud-Ssv_pQ">Spotify</a>, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL5QAQBV5zCqVsMqk5UH-LVYuNmM4i6pgW">YouTube</a>, <a href="https://overcast.fm/itunes1454097755/naval">Overcast</a>, <a href="https://www.google.com/podcasts?feed=aHR0cHM6Ly9uYXZhbC5saWJzeW4uY29tL3Jzcw">Google</a>, <a href="https://www.breaker.audio/naval">Breaker</a> and every other damn app on the planet.</p>
<p>The title is a little cheesy. It should really be called “How to Create Wealth.” But that doesn’t have the same oomph.</p>
<p><b>This time it’s different</b></p>
<p>This podcast is different than other advice on getting rich. It’s free. There are no ads. It’s abstract. Our goal is to make timeless material, not to sell ads or keep you listening.</p>
<p>Each episode is only a few minutes long, covering a single topic and there’s no fluff. We use <a href="https://www.descript.com">Descript</a> to chop up, re-arrange and organize the interviews before publication. You don’t have to fast forward through 10 minutes of intro to get to the good stuff.</p>
<p>We publish clean, organized transcripts so you can read each episode if you like.</p>
<p>The episodes are edited for sound quality by our killer sound guy and filler words are removed (um like basically). If you hate the sound quality on the first few episodes, hang on, it gets a lot better as we improve our mics and switch to <a href="https://www.zencastr.com/">Zencastr</a> in later episodes.</p>
<p>Everyone on the planet should hear this podcast, whether they want to build a 1-person cashflow business or they want want to build a multi-billion dollar company with a team.</p>
<p>You can do us a favor by sharing it with someone who needs to hear it.</p>

<h2>
<a href="https://venturehacks.com/writing" rel="bookmark" title="Permanent Link: How to write like the great entrepreneurs">
                            How to write like the great entrepreneurs                            </a>
</h2>
</div> <!-- /.postHead -->
<div class="entry">
<div class="postInfo">
<h4>
<span class="postAuthor"><a href="/about">Nivi</a></span>
<span class="middot">
                                     ·
                                    </span>
<span class="postTime">
                                     April 22nd, 2019                                    </span>
<!-- &middot; <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/books" rel="category tag">Books</a>, <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/design" rel="category tag">Design</a>, <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/negotiation" rel="category tag">Negotiation</a>, <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/persuasion" rel="category tag">Persuasion</a> -->
</h4>
</div> <!-- /postInfo -->
<p>Entrepreneurs are the best business writers in the world. If you can’t write, you can’t raise money. Or recruit. Or sell. </p>
<p>I don’t know a single great entrepreneur who isn’t a great writer.</p>
<p>Good business writing is clear, compelling and concise. Read <a href="https://www.apple.com/hotnews/thoughts-on-flash/">Steve Jobs</a>, <a href="https://www.tesla.com/en_CA/blog/staying-public">Elon Musk</a> and <a href="http://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/letters.html">Warren Buffett</a> (though they should have used half the words).</p>
<p>Here’s what I send my friends when they ask for writing tips:</p>
<ol><li>Writing is a customer service problem.</li><li>Pretend you’re sending an email.</li><li>Sum it up in a tweet.</li><li>Read it on your phone. <br/></li><li>Don’t write your thought process.<br/></li><li>Start with a summary.</li><li>Writing is rewriting. </li><li>Delete half the words.</li><li>Avoid adjectives.</li><li>Scrutinize every word for bias.</li><li>Kill your darlings.</li><li>Use persuasion checklists.<br/></li><li>Skim Strunk &amp; White.</li><li>Break the rules once you learn the rules.</li><li>Writing is a design problem.</li></ol>
<p><strong>1. Business writing is a customer service problem.</strong> You’re not the star—the reader is. Help them get what they want, as quickly and effectively as possible. They might want to solve a problem. They might want to be persuaded. Give ’em the goods.</p>
<p><strong>2. Pretend you’re sending an email.</strong> Or a Slack message. It will calm your mind and yield better writing. <br/></p>
<p><strong>3. Sum it up in a tweet.</strong> If the tweet isn’t compelling, the rest isn’t compelling. The ideal tweet absolves the reader from reading further. Sequoia says, “Summarize the company’s business on the back of a business card.”</p>
<p><strong>4. Email it to yourself and read it on your phone.</strong> You’ll see the words with fresh eyes, as if someone else wrote them. This will force you to keep it short and simple.</p>
<p><strong>5. Don’t write your thought process.</strong> The final draft shouldn’t mimic the path you took to come up with the idea. Instead, start the piece with a conclusion and make your best case.<br/></p>
<p><strong>6. Start with a summary.</strong> A good summary absolves the reader from reading further. But they will still want to.</p>
<p><strong>7. Writing is rewriting. </strong>Write down your thoughts in a stream of consciousness. Don’t get hung up on diction. Then spend most of your time rewriting and reorganizing—<a href="https://venturehacks.com/articles/details">sweat the details</a>. I’m still rewriting posts days after I’ve published them.</p>
<p><strong>8. Delete half the words.</strong> Say more with less. That’s good customer service. “If I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter.”</p>
<p><strong>9. Avoid adjectives. </strong>Use numbers instead. An adjective is an admission that you don’t know the number.</p>
<p><strong>10. Scrutinize every word for bias and rhetoric.</strong> Are they an ‘unruly mob’ or ‘patriots’? Perhaps neither—just call them by their name. Argue the other side of every word, at least to yourself.  Learn more about <a href="https://www.google.com/search?q=media+bias+techniques&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8&amp;hl=en-us&amp;client=safari">bias</a>. <br/></p>
<p><strong>11. Kill your darlings. </strong>Delete beautiful ideas and phrases if they don’t help the customer solve their problem.<br/></p>
<p><strong>12. Use persuasion checklists like CLASSR and SUCCES. </strong>See the Appendix for details. </p>
<p><strong>13. Skim </strong><a href="https://www.google.com/search?q=strunk+and+white&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8&amp;hl=en-us&amp;client=safari"><strong>Strunk &amp; White</strong></a><strong> once in a while.</strong> You don’t need to read the whole book at once. Also read <a href="http://dilbertblog.typepad.com/the_dilbert_blog/2007/06/the_day_you_bec.html">The Day You Became a Better Writer</a>.</p>
<p><strong>14. Break the rules once you learn the rules.</strong> Write in your authentic voice. Tell a story. Use adjectives! Learn which <a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;ei=tlG3XNrsEoaUsgWC6pjwCA&amp;q=never+split+the+difference+chris+voss&amp;oq=never+split+the+difference+chris+voss&amp;gs_l=psy-ab.3..0l7j0i22i30.1811.5848..5944...9.0..0.129.1644.13j5....2..0....1..gws-wiz.......0i71j0i22i10i30j33i160j0i13j0i10.5-mNroru0fc">word choices</a> unlock action. But first learn how to write clearly and concisely.</p>
<p><strong>15. Writing is a design problem.</strong> Example: never use the idiom of ‘the former or the latter.’ It forces the reader to go back and figure out what you’re referring to. </p>
<p>Learn design by reading <a href="https://www.edwardtufte.com/tufte/books_vdqi">Tufte</a>, <a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;q=a+pattern+language&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8">A Pattern Language</a> and <a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;ei=-VG3XMreBMvUtQWty7-IAw&amp;q=don%27t+make+me+think+steve+krug&amp;oq=don%27t+make+me+think+steve+krug&amp;gs_l=psy-ab.3..46i275j0l4j0i22i10i30j0i22i30l2.3027.4430..4558...0.0..0.105.1071.6j5....3..0....1..gws-wiz.......0i71j46.J5R1LhQlaSk">Don’t Make Me Think</a>. Dieter Rams: “Indifference towards people and the reality in which they live is actually the one and only cardinal sin in design.”</p>
<p style="text-align:left"><strong>Appendix</strong></p>
<p>Consider adding a sentence for each of the <a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;q=influence+cialidni&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8">CLASSR</a> persuasion techniques: commitment, liking, authority, scarcity, social proof and reciprocity. Here’s a joke example by Victor Ghitescu: </p>
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><p>“Learn more about CLASSR by reading <a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;q=influence+cialidni&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8">Influence</a> by Cialdini. Do it because you like books that make you smarter. Do it for me, I’m an expert on this. The world’s best salespeople have all read it. Do it before the whole world finds out about it. You can thank me later.”</p></blockquote>
<p>Make sure your writing is simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, stories (<a href="https://149348552.v2.pressablecdn.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Screen-Shot-2018-01-02-at-19.37.23.png">SUCCES</a> from <a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;q=made+to+stick+heath&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8">Made to Stick</a>):</p>
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><p>“I believe that this nation should commit itself to achieving the goal, before this decade is out, of landing a man on the moon and returning him safely to the earth.”</p></blockquote>

<h2>
<a href="https://venturehacks.com/11-lessons" rel="bookmark" title="Permanent Link: 11 angel investing lessons">
                            11 angel investing lessons                            </a>
</h2>
</div> <!-- /.postHead -->
<div class="entry">
<div class="postInfo">
<h4>
<span class="postAuthor"><a href="/about">Nivi</a></span>
<span class="middot">
                                     ·
                                    </span>
<span class="postTime">
                                     April 17th, 2019                                    </span>
<!-- &middot; <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/angels" rel="category tag">Angels</a>, <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/investing" rel="category tag">Investing</a>, <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/negotiation" rel="category tag">Negotiation</a>, <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/valuation" rel="category tag">Valuation</a> -->
</h4>
</div> <!-- /postInfo -->
<p><em><a href="http://spearhead.co">Spearhead</a> asked me to write a post on angel investing when they first launched. Here’s a slightly updated version—most of the wisdom is from <a href="http://twitter.com/naval">Naval</a></em>.</p>
<p>Charlie Munger says investing requires a <a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;q=latticework+munger&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8">latticework</a> of mental models. Here are 11 lessons for your angel investing lattice:</p>
<ol><li>If you can’t decide, the answer is no.</li><li>Proprietary dealfow means ‘they want you’.</li><li>Investing takes years to learn, but improves for a lifetime.</li><li>Valuation matters: you will have to pass on future greats.</li><li>Back $0B companies.</li><li>Judgment is important but overrated.</li><li>Invest only in technology.</li><li>Some of the best investors have no opinions.</li><li>Incentives make for bad investing advice.</li><li>Play fantasy football.</li><li>Power beats contracts.</li></ol>
<h3>1. If you can’t decide, the answer is no</h3>
<p>If you can’t decide on an investment, the answer is no. For all practical purposes, there are an infinite number of investments out there, so pass. </p>
<p>That doesn’t mean you won’t regret it. But the next investment is just as good <em>a priori</em>.</p>
<p>Your experience and judgement is only going to get better by the time you see the next deal.</p>
<h3>2. Proprietary dealflow means ‘they want you’</h3>
<p>Nobody thinks they have a shortage of dealflow. The hard problem is getting your money into the startups you want. The company has to want you over other investors. </p>
<p>Without ‘they want you,’ you will get cut out of good investments and end up with adverse selection of weaker companies. It’s okay to pass on investments, but you don’t want them to pass on you.</p>
<p>Missing out on a few investments can mean losing all your money because of the <a href="http://blakemasters.com/post/21869934240/peter-thiels-cs183-startup-class-7-notes-essay">power law</a> returns of investing: the top deal in a good portfolio returns as much as deals 2 through N combined. If you miss out on the top deal, you’re going to miss out on most of your returns.</p>
<p>You never want to hear, “I will come to you if I don’t get money from Sequoia.”</p>
<h3>3. Investing takes years to learn, but improves for a lifetime</h3>
<p>Get started with angel investing now. It takes years to learn and longer to see returns. </p>
<p>You want to invest in 30 companies at a minimum–that takes time. Start with small investments because your later ones will get better as you gain expertise and brand. So your returns will take even longer.</p>
<p>Investing takes a long time to learn, but it is one of the few professions that you can improve until the day you die.</p>
<h3>4. Valuation matters: you will have to pass on future greats</h3>
<p>You can’t build a portfolio of pre-traction companies at $8-10M pre-money and expect to make a venture return. On occasion, you can make an exception, but you can’t do all of your investments at this price.</p>
<p>You will have to pass on great teams because the valuation is too high. You will have to pass on future iconic technology companies because the price is too high. But passing at a $40M pre-money lets you take 10 shots on goal with unknown companies at $4M pre-money.</p>
<p>You can’t negotiate valuation unless you’re investing 1/3 to 1/2 of the round. Or if you’re the first check in the company. Start the negotiation by saying, “I like you but I can’t make the valuation work, but I would invest if the valuation were X.”</p>
<p>Despite high valuations, it’s still possible to make money in angel investing. If you can’t make money in tech, you can’t make money anywhere. </p>
<p><em><strong>Anecdotal valuation data</strong></em></p>
<p>Valuations for pre-traction companies between 2005-2010 were $1-5M pre-money for the first non-friends-and-family round. Funds that invested during this time period made 4x-100x returns.</p>
<p>These valuations moved to $4-6M pre-money after 2010, with some demo days in the $8-10M range. This likely cut returns by 2/3 or more.</p>
<p>Play with <a href="https://angel.co/valuations">valuations tool</a> on AngelList.</p>
<h3>5. Back $0B companies</h3>
<p>To quote Vinod Khosla, invest in “<a href="https://www.google.com/search?client=safari&amp;rls=en&amp;q=vinod+khosla+$0B&amp;ie=UTF-8&amp;oe=UTF-8">$0B companies</a>” that could be worth $1B tomorrow. </p>
<p>Focus your attention only on companies with the potential for a 100-1000x return. Otherwise, pass.</p>
<p>Without these large exits, your portfolio will not achieve a venture return. </p>
<h3>6. Judgment about markets is important but overrated</h3>
<p>Some markets are obviously bad and should be avoided. But judgment about markets is less important than you think, because there is so much luck and randomness involved. Companies can do hard pivots into new markets (Twitter, Slack and Instagram).</p>
<p>Judgment is not about doing a lot of research, digging and homework. By the time you figure it out, you will have missed the deal. Instead, learn a few markets really well. </p>
<p>Of course, you will learn about new markets over time. But learn a few markets really well. Buy all the products and try them. </p>
<p>Find the best scientists in the market and invest in them. They can help you with research on your next investment; this is an unfair advantage. </p>
<p>Read research papers then call the grad students who wrote them. Waiting to learn about new markets on TechCrunch is too slow.</p>
<h3>7. Invest only in technology </h3>
<p>The best returns come from investing in technology companies. Avoid companies that don’t develop meaningful technology (either software or hardware).</p>
<p>The 5 largest companies in the S&amp;P 500 (Apple, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Facebook) are all technology companies. The largest private companies are also technology companies. </p>
<p>There are exceptions like Dollar Shave Club. Their early investors had good returns. But, as a rule of thumb, you should only invest in technology.</p>
<h3>8. Some of the best investors have no opinions</h3>
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><p><em>“I have no idea what’s hot. But I’m certainly always listening. Big Dumbo ears. Just listening.” – </em><a href="https://www.sequoiacap.com/people/doug-leone/"><em>Doug Leone</em></a><em>, Sequoia</em></p></blockquote>
<p>Some of the best investors on the planet have no strong opinions about a particular business. They try not to project into the future, so they can listen intently in the present.</p>
<p>Almost any entrepreneur will be smarter than them in their market. The investor’s job is to listen and decide whether the founders are smart, honest, and hard-working. </p>
<p>These investors don’t fall in love with a business. When it comes time to do a new round, they re-evaluate the business from scratch and ignore sunk costs.</p>
<p>If you’re thinking about all the great things you could do if you were running the business, you’re going down the wrong path: you’re not running the business.</p>
<p>If you are telling the entrepreneur what to do, don’t invest. Thinking like an investor is different than thinking like an entrepreneur who is determined to make a business work.</p>
<h3>9. Incentives make for bad investing advice</h3>
<p>Incentives influence the advice you get from VCs, lawyers, incubators, and everybody else. Everyone serves their own interests first. The best source for angel investing advice is other angels and founders.</p>
<p>People are generally well-meaning but, in the words of Upton Sinclair, “It is difficult to get a man to understand something, when his salary depends upon his not understanding it!”</p>
<h3>10. Play fantasy football</h3>
<p>Build your instincts by looking at startups without investing. Your instincts are what you really use to make investment decisions. </p>
<p>In the old days, you had to work at a VC firm to see dealflow. You had to make a few investments and lose money before getting good judgment. John Doerr called this “<a href="https://www.forbes.com/forbes/2005/0704/039.html#14bd69604fe0">crashing a fighter jet</a>.”  First you lose $25M, then you have some judgment.</p>
<p>Now you can get judgment without crashing the fighter jet. You can see dealflow from your friends, your incubator, demo days, and AngelList. </p>
<p>You need a lot of data to build up your instincts. Track your fantasy portfolio and <a href="https://www.bvp.com/anti-portfolio/">anti-portfolio</a>. Write down what you like and dislike about each deal and see how your judgment develops over time. </p>
<h3>11. Power beats contracts</h3>
<p>Contracts can be renegotiated. You will be pressured to renegotiate your investment by founders and VCs. If you’re alone, you won’t have the power to fight back.</p>
<p>Contracts are written for worst-case scenarios, so people can’t outright steal your money. Suing people is bad for your dealflow. So real-world decisions are usually based on power.</p>
<p>If you’re the only seed investor in a round, you can get screwed. There aren’t enough co-investors to make a ruckus if the company wants to:</p>
<ul><li>Recap and start over</li><li>Raise the cap on your convertible note</li><li>Give your pro rata to a new investor</li></ul>
<p>If you’re alone, you won’t have the power to fight back. The startup and their new investors can pressure you to renegotiate. So don’t be a herd animal when making an investment decision, but move with a pack when you do. </p>

<a href="https://venturehacks.com/angel-3" rel="bookmark" title="Permanent Link: How to be an angel investor, Part 3">
                            How to be an angel investor, Part 3                            </a>
</h2>
</div> <!-- /.postHead -->
<div class="entry">
<div class="postInfo">
<h4>
<span class="postAuthor"><a href="/about">Nivi</a></span>
<span class="middot">
                                     ·
                                    </span>
<span class="postTime">
                                     September 11th, 2014                                    </span>
<!-- &middot; <a href="https://venturehacks.com/topics/angels" rel="category tag">Angels</a> -->
</h4>
</div> <!-- /postInfo -->
<p><em>This guest post is by <a href="http://tyler.is">Tyler Willis</a>, an entrepreneur and angel investor. You can learn more about him on <a href="http://angel.co/tylerwillis">AngelList</a>.</em></p>
<p>For several interesting macro-economic reasons [1], more and more people are becoming angel investors.</p>
<p>This is a good thing – it allows more investors to participate in a high-growth (but high-risk) area of our economy. That said, investing in private companies is very different from investing in public companies. </p>
<p>People who are just getting started in angel investing should get comfortable with the inherent risks and learn the strategies required to be successful angel investors. Without doing this, you run the very real risk of losing every dollar you invest in this market.</p>
<p>Two years ago, my first company was acquired by Oracle and four members of our early team, including myself, became part time angel investors. Before I started investing, I tried to learn as much as possible about angel investing. In all of the things I read and people I talked to, two posts stood out as particularly helpful: Paul Graham’s post <a href="http://www.paulgraham.com/angelinvesting.html">How to Be an Angel Investor</a> and Naval and Nivi’s <a href="http://venturehacks.com/articles/angel">How to be an angel investor, Part 2</a>. These posts were helpful because they did away with the inside baseball and tried to present a comprehensive overview for a novice investor.</p>
<p>This year, several of my friends became accredited and asked for my advice on angel investing. Inspired by the opportunity to help them, I looked back on my first year of investing and tried to tie all of those lessons up in a comprehensive overview–think of it as Angel Investing 101. Drawing on the previous two posts, I called the presentation <a href="https://www.slideshare.net/tylerwillis/growing-wings-37622237">How to Be an Angel Investor, Part 3</a>.</p>
<p></p><center><iframe allowfullscreen="" frameborder="0" height="356" marginheight="0" marginwidth="0" scrolling="no" src="//www.slideshare.net/slideshow/embed_code/37622237" style="border:1px solid #CCC; border-width:1px; margin-bottom:5px; max-width: 100%;" width="427"> </iframe> <p></p>
<div style="margin-bottom:5px"> <strong> <a href="https://www.slideshare.net/tylerwillis/growing-wings-37622237" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank" title="How to be an Angel Investor, Part 3">How to Be an Angel Investor, Part 3</a> </strong></div>
<p></p></center><p></p>
<p>Success as an angel investor boils down to whether you can pick the right companies. The information in this presentation won’t make you a successful angel investor on it’s own, but it can help you avoid the common pitfalls and develop a better understanding of how this market works and whether you’re ready for it.</p>
<p>Because these investments are illiquid, you won’t know for many years whether you are doing a good job of picking the right companies. My best advice is to focus first on learning as much as you can so you can avoid common pitfalls. Once you’ve done that, budget money you can afford to lose and start slowly. You’re generally going to be better served by spreading your initial investment budget over several years, rather than trying to invest it all in a short period of time.</p>
<p>If you find this useful, I intend to create more free resources for angel investors (including video interviews with successful investors sharing their best advice). If you’re interested in more information like this, check out <a href="http://www.adviceforangels.com">adviceforangels.com</a>.</p>
<p></p>
<p>—</p>
<p>[1] Broadly, we’re creating a much more affluent upper middle class. Tyler Cowen’s book <a href="http://www.amazon.com/Average-Is-Over-Powering-Stagnation/dp/0525953736">Average Is Over</a> is a good read about this if you’re interested.</p>
<p>Specifically, there are a few big changes that have created more angel investors. </p>
<ul>
<li>Startups provide equity compensation by default, so the influx of new startups means that more people employed under this model.</li>
<li>Companies are staying private longer, which incentivizes investors to move to private markets in search of good returns.</li>
<li>It seems likely that startups will share more equity with employees than they have historically (this is a prediction, but we’re starting to see early examples of this)</li>
</ul>
