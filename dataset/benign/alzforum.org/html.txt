<h1 class="entry-title"><a href="https://www.alzforum.org/news/research-news/imaging-model-locates-epicenter-disease-predicts-atrophy">MRI Evidence Reveals Past, Future Path of Disease</a></h1>
<span class="rdf-meta element-hidden" content="MRI Evidence Reveals Past, Future Path of Disease" property="dc:title"></span><span class="rdf-meta element-hidden" content="0" datatype="xsd:integer" property="sioc:num_replies"></span>
<!--  Spotlight Content -->
<p class="entry-image" data-has-image="true" data-image-src="https://www.alzforum.org/sites/default/files/1018_Prediction_spotlight.jpg" title=""><img alt="" src="https://www.alzforum.org/sites/default/files/1018_Prediction_spotlight.jpg"/></p>
<div class="entry-summary">
<p>Within any given neurodegenerative disease, patterns of brain atrophy may be similar, but they are still heterogeneous. This makes it hard to predict which areas of a person’s brain will deteriorate in the future. A new model uses baseline structural MRI to predict both where the disease likely started and where it will go next. This model allowed researchers to predict which brain regions were most at risk for future decline in individuals with either bvFTD or svPPA. The results may point to a new biomarker for early stage clinical trials.</p>
</div>

<h1 class="entry-title"><a href="https://www.alzforum.org/news/research-news/could-getting-enough-rest-extend-your-life">Could Getting Enough REST Extend Your Life?</a></h1>
<span class="rdf-meta element-hidden" content="Could Getting Enough REST Extend Your Life?" property="dc:title"></span><span class="rdf-meta element-hidden" content="0" datatype="xsd:integer" property="sioc:num_replies"></span>
<!--  Spotlight Content -->
<p class="entry-image" data-has-image="true" data-image-src="https://www.alzforum.org/sites/default/files/10.18_Yankner-REST_and_longevity_spotlight_2.jpg" title=""><img alt="" src="https://www.alzforum.org/sites/default/files/10.18_Yankner-REST_and_longevity_spotlight_2.jpg"/></p>
<div class="entry-summary">
<p>We all know the brain needs rest to age well, but now it turns out it may need REST too. This neuroprotective transcription factor quiets excitatory neuronal activity. Its levels rise in the brain during healthy aging. In roundworms, boosting a REST ortholog lengthened lifespan, while suppressing it shortened life. The data hint that adjusting the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neuronal signaling could help keep the brain young.</p>
</div>

<h1 class="entry-title"><a href="https://www.alzforum.org/news/research-news/it-bleeds-new-mini-brains-sport-functioning-blood-vessels">It Bleeds! New Mini-Brain Sports Functioning Blood Vessels</a></h1>
<span class="rdf-meta element-hidden" content="It Bleeds! New Mini-Brain Sports Functioning Blood Vessels" property="dc:title"></span><span class="rdf-meta element-hidden" content="0" datatype="xsd:integer" property="sioc:num_replies"></span>
<!--  Spotlight Content -->
<p class="entry-image" data-has-image="true" data-image-src="https://www.alzforum.org/sites/default/files/10.10_Vascular_organoids_spot.jpg" title=""><img alt="" src="https://www.alzforum.org/sites/default/files/10.10_Vascular_organoids_spot.jpg"/></p>
<div class="entry-summary">
<p>Pea-sized clumps of cortical cells grown in a dish mimic some aspects of the brain, but without a vascular system, neurons inside these organoids get sick and die. Now, scientists have found a way to encourage blood vessels to grow in these little blobs, which keeps the neurons healthy for months. Conveniently, cells lining the vessels form tight junctions, similar to those of the blood-brain barrier. The vascularized organoids could enable in vitro study of barrier function, researchers believe.​</p>
</div>
