<a href="/journal_entry/8879016"><span class="subheading_text">Mollie Fancher: The Brooklyn Enigma</span></a>
[Featured Site Content]
</div>
<div class="newsfeed_date">Oct 24, 2019, 07:06am
</div>
<a href="/VampireFreaks"><img alt="" class="newsfeed_entry_thumb" src="//pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/50845677_s.jpg"/></a>
<a class="newsfeed_entry_username" href="/VampireFreaks">VampireFreaks</a>
<div class="newsfeed_entry_txt message_limited">
<img class="entry_pic" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/contentpics.vampirefreaks.com/journal/8/88/887/8879016/8879016.jpg"/>
<div class="message_div_limited">
The Victorian era is known for having produced all sorts of new belief systems, religious philosophies, and thought leaders as people struggled to understand the rapidly changing world around them. I've <a href="https://vampirefreaks.com/journal_entry/8869354" target="_blank">written before</a> about the rise of Spiritualism, a religious movement that grew out of the Victorian craze for communicating with the dead. If you're familiar with the topic at all, you've probably heard about sensational mediums such as the Fox Sisters who held large public seances and attracted the attention of many prominent members of society. But less often talked about is the related phenomenon of fasting girls. Somewhat less showy than the seance-holding mediums, fasting girls were generally young adolescents who claimed to subsist for extended periods of time on very little or no food, which allowed them to access mystical knowledge or power. Similar stories are found in medieval legends about several Catholic saints, but in the 19th century, these miracle girls seemed to be popping up everywhere throughout America and the United Kingdom. One of the most famous of these fasting girls was a young Brooklynite named Mollie Fancher.<br/>
<br/>
Molly experienced several tragedies early on in life. At 16 years old, she fell from a horse and hit her head, after which she regularly experienced fainting spells and violent headaches. Only a year later, her head injury was compounded significantly when her skirt got caught in a trolley car and she was dragged along the pavement for nearly a block before the driver noticed and stopped. She survived, but her injuries caused some rather unusual effects. The story goes that in the initial months after her accident, she recovered enough to be able to walk around the house. Then one day she suddenly started acting bizarre: she would stand on her toes and "spin like a top" or grab onto her ankles and "roll like a hoop" across the kitchen floor. Members of her household carried her to her bed, where she would ultimately spend the next 50 years of her life.<br/>
<br/>
During these bedridden decades, Mollie was known to abstain from food, fall into trances, and demonstrate feats of clairvoyance. She could read folded and sealed letters by merely running her hand over the paper, could describe what far off friends and family were wearing and doing, could predict thunderstorms, and could tell who was at the door when the doorbell rang—all without leaving her bed. Periodically, she would fall into trances in which her whole body would be paralyzed, except for her left hand. During these trances, however, she was amazingly productive! With her eyes shut and using only that one hand, Mollie would produce complex embroidery and crochet, craft delicate wax flowers, and write over 6,500 letters. Many believed that while in these trances, Mollie traveled to heaven and back, communing with angels and the spirits of the dead. Visitors would come from all over to witness this medical miracle, seek mystical knowledge, and purchase some of her handiwork as souvenirs. <br/>
<br/>
As Mollie grew older, the public began to lose interest in her and Mollie started eating again and living a somewhat more normal life as an invalid. However, she still had a small number of devotees who came out to celebrate the 50th anniversary of her confinement in 1916. She died just a few months later. Mollie Fancher is buried in Green-Wood Cemetery in Brooklyn under a headstone that claims "Mollie Fancher knew the secret of life."<br/>

<div class="subheading">
<a href="/journal_entry/8878869"><span class="subheading_text">How to Celebrate Halloween All Month Long</span></a>
[Featured Site Content]
</div>
<div class="newsfeed_date">Oct 17, 2019, 06:58am
</div>
<a href="/VampireFreaks"><img alt="" class="newsfeed_entry_thumb" src="//pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/50845677_s.jpg"/></a>
<a class="newsfeed_entry_username" href="/VampireFreaks">VampireFreaks</a>
<div class="newsfeed_entry_txt message_limited">
<img class="entry_pic" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/contentpics.vampirefreaks.com/journal/8/88/887/8878869/8878869.jpg"/>
<div class="message_div_limited">
Traditionally, Halloween has just been a one-night affair, celebrated by donning costumes and trick-or-treating on the evening of October 31. Sometimes people expand the holiday to include the evening before Halloween (known as Mischief Night) or the nearest weekend. But since Christmas season always seems to last at least a month long these days, why can't we do the same for Halloween? Here are some ideas for activities you can do throughout October to keep in the spooky spirit for the entire month:<br/>
<br/>
<b>1. Get a Halloween advent calendar.</b> Generally, advent calendars are a Christmas thing, but if Christmas supplies and decorations are going to start creeping into stores before October is even over, I think we can appropriate a few of its traditions for Halloween. You can find a wide variety of custom-made Halloween advent calendars on sites like Etsy, and a few on Amazon, as well. Or you can make your own! Decorated with ghosts and haunted houses, these calendars feature little pockets to store candy in. Get a small taste of Halloween each day of October as you count down to the big night!<br/>
<br/>
<b>2. Go pumpkin picking.</b> Pumpkin picking is both a fun harvest-time activity in itself and a great way to stock up on Halloween decorations. Find a farm near you that has a pumpkin patch, gather a group of friends, and make a day out of it! Comb the field for the perfect pumpkin to make your jack-o-lantern, or just grab a bunch of mini pumpkins and gourds to make into a gorgeous autumnal display.<br/>
<br/>
<b>3. Decorate your pumpkins.</b> Once you've picked a pumpkin (or just picked one up from the store), it's time to decorate it! I wrote a piece last year full of <a href="https://vampirefreaks.com/journal_entry/8863630" target="_blank">pumpkin-carving tips</a> to help you make the perfect jack-o-lantern. If carving up a pumpkin sounds too messy to you, you can also paint it instead! <br/>
<br/>
<b>4. Go on a hayride.</b> Hayrides are another traditional fall activity that you can also find at local farms. Many of these are Halloween themed, with terrifying monsters that jump out at you as you ride along. But others are just a simple way to appreciate the harvest season.<br/>
<br/>
<b>5. Visit a local haunted house or haunted trail.</b> Visiting haunted houses or trails is the perfect way to get your scare on, and many of these are open all October long! These attractions can vary widely in fright-level, so even if you're a bit of a scaredy cat or have small children in tow, do some research and you can probably find a haunted house or trail that's perfect for you!<br/>
<br/>
<b>6. Build a Halloween gingerbread house.</b> Sure, this is another activity traditionally associated with Christmas, but Trader Joe's, Target, and other stores have recently started selling Halloween-themed gingerbread house kits. These kits come with things like orange and black icing, bat- and bone-shaped candies, and a little gingerbread ghost to haunt your house. See one such house that my sister built in the photo above.<br/>
<br/>
<b>7. Host a Halloween paint night.</b> I'm sure you're familiar with the new trend of paint-and-sip nights, where you get together with a group of friends either at a bar or at someone's house to hang out and drink while you all paint the same picture. Well, there are plenty of tutorials for these paintings on Youtube, and many of them are Halloween-themed! Here are a few options that I've found: <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qbKwfiaJpFI&amp;fbclid=IwAR2OE-5LGvUZK9Ql1xTrTuTzQXG1AdiodZyQJSzLe6V1eN3fI10iyyb_iaw" target="_blank">Spooky Cat Painting tutorial</a>; <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=veh_weZow7A&amp;fbclid=IwAR1QF6B5yeqQr6J524rPbK2xv3yuZrQ8nfHRhmfKJXTtPL_ikPS1TwyB_7k" target="_blank">Stacked Pumpkins Painting tutorial</a>; and <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fTg7h4XIZgk&amp;fbclid=IwAR3fBRF0d_bMzi3W40E72AtVZ8yXqbM7t68ODwKoJPczKmHdvDqWCnkjLJc" target="_blank">Raven Painting tutorial</a>.<br/>
<br/>
8. Have a Halloween movie marathon.&lt;/b&gt; Getting a group of friends together for a movie marathon is a great way to get in the Halloween mood. You can make a watch-list of the scariest horror movies you can think of, or get nostalgic with all of the Halloween-themed movies from your childhood, like <i>Halloweentown</i> and <i>Hocus Pocus</i>. Last week, I also wrote up some suggestions for <a href="https://vampirefreaks.com/journal_entry/8878744" target="_blank">horror movies inspired by Gothic literature</a>. If you get a long enough list together, you can keep your movie marathon going throughout the month.<br/>
<br/>
<b>9. Make mulled cider.</b> Hot, spiced cider is one of the most delicious aspects of fall. And it's not that hard to make! Generally, all you need is a large pot, fresh apple juice or cider, and spices like cinnamon, cloves, star anise, and orange peels. Check out this <a href="https://www.foodnetwork.com/recipes/ina-garten/hot-mulled-cider-recipe-1914496/" target="_blank">very basic recipe</a> from Food Network.<br/>
<br/>
<b>10. Go on a ghost tour.</b> If strangers jumping out at you at haunted attractions isn't your thing, you may prefer a more sedate, but equally spooky, ghost tour. The really good ones are equal parts history lesson and tales of terror. Going on ghost tours can help you learn about the local history of your own town or can be a great place to get to know somewhere new, if you find yourself traveling during October. If you can't find anyone leading ghost tours in your area, you can always put one together yourself by researching ghost sightings and scary folklore in your area, then gathering friends to visit the supposedly haunted sites for yourselves!<br/>
<br/>
What are you planning to do this Halloween season? How long do you celebrate for? Share your thoughts in the comments!<br/>

<a href="/journal_entry/8878744"><span class="subheading_text">Halloween Movie Marathon of Literary Adaptations</span></a>
[Featured Site Content]
</div>
<div class="newsfeed_date">Oct 10, 2019, 06:55am

</div>
<a href="/VampireFreaks"><img alt="" class="newsfeed_entry_thumb" src="//pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/50845677_s.jpg"/></a>
<a class="newsfeed_entry_username" href="/VampireFreaks">VampireFreaks</a>
<div class="newsfeed_entry_txt message_limited">
<img class="entry_pic" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/contentpics.vampirefreaks.com/journal/8/88/887/8878744/8878744.jpg"/>
<div class="message_div_limited">
October is well underway, and what better way to get into the spooky spirit than with a horror movie marathon? Now I'm not so much a movie person as a book person, but I do try to keep up with the film adaptations of my favorite works of Gothic literature. Below are a few of my favorite literary adaptations that would make great additions to any Halloween movie night:<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>1. <i>Rebecca</i> (1940) </b></font><br/>
<br/>
This romantic thriller is one of Alfred Hitchcock's most celebrated films. Based on a novel of the same name by Daphne du Maurier, the film centers on an unnamed young woman (Joan Fontaine) who marries the wealthy widower Maxim de Winter (Laurence Olivier) and moves into his fancy estate, Manderley. Almost immediately, the new Mrs. de Winter feels out of place. The whole mansion is full of reminders of her new husband's first wife, Rebecca--including a creepy housekeeper named Mrs. Danvers who remains obsessively devoted to her dead mistress. The new bride fears that she will be forever living under the shadow of her predecessor, but it turns out that the history of Manderley is much darker than she could have guessed. If you're only adding one Hitchcock film to your marathon this year, I highly this Gothic masterpiece.<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>2. <i>The Innocents</i> (1961) </b></font><br/>
<br/>
This psychological horror film by British director and producer Jack Clayton is inspired by Henry James' classic novella <i>The Turn of the Screw</i>. In the film, an inexperienced governess (played by Deborah Kerr) takes a job looking after two young children at a country estate called Bly. When the children begin exhibiting strange behavior, the governess becomes convinced that they are being possessed by the ghosts of two former servants that died at the estate. But are there actual ghosts endangering the children or is the governess just losing her mind? Jack Clayton brilliantly captures the haunting ambiguity and chilling atmosphere of James' story.<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>3. <i>The Woman in Black</i> (2012) </b></font><br/>
<br/>
Want to see Harry Potter all grown up and in a horror film? Daniel Radcliffe stars in this adaptation of a popular supernatural horror novel of the 1980s, <i>The Woman in Black</i> by Susan Hill. Radcliffe's character, a lawyer named Arthur Kipps, is sent to retrieve documents from the eerie Eel Marsh House, an isolated estate that can only be reached during low tide. Kipps hears odd noises and sees spectral figures at the estate, while tragedy after tragedy seems to befall the surrounding village. Slowly, he uncovers the story of the ghostly Woman in Black and how her presence relates to the deaths of local children. Kipps attempts to set the spirit to rest before any more children die, but will his efforts be enough? Though it differs significantly from the original novel, this creepy tale filled with ghosts galore is the perfect thing to send chills down your spine on Halloween.<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>4. <i>My Cousin Rachel</i> (2017) </b></font><br/>
<br/>
I know there's already one Daphne du Maurier story on this list, but I can't pass up the chance to tell everyone about this newer adaptation of one of her lesser-known novels, <i>My Cousin Rachel</i>. This British film stars Rachel Weisz as the beautiful widow Rachel. The story's protagonist, Philip (played by Sam Clafin), first learns of Rachel when his cousin Ambrose writes home with the surprising announcement that he has met his "cousin Rachel" while abroad and married her. When Ambrose dies unexpectedly in Italy, Philip suspects foul play. Rachel comes to stay at Ambrose's old estate and Philip plans to confront her, but instead becomes smitten by her beauty and forgets his suspicions. Philip and Rachel begin a tempestuous relationship, but when things sour he is reminded of his earlier misgivings. Is Rachel really a murderer or the sweet woman she appears to be? Are Philips feelings clouding his judgment? This faithful adaptation of du Maurier's novel is a chilling reminder that sometimes uncertainty can be more frightening than obvious dangers.<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>5. <i>We Have Always Lived in the Castle</i> (2018) </b></font><br/>
<br/>
The final movie on this list is one that came out just last year, based on a Shirley Jackson novel. While Shirley Jackson's most famous work, <i>The Haunting of Hill House</i>, has been adapted many, many times (most recently as a loosely inspired Netflix series), this is the first time that <i>We Have Always Lived in the Castle</i> was adapted for the big screen. Taissa Farmiga and Alexandra Daddario star as Merricat and Constance Blackwood, two sisters that live in an isolated old estate with their convalescent uncle. Ever since the rest of the Blackwood family was mysteriously murdered six years before, Constance refuses to leave the estate and Merricat copes by placing protective spells around the house. The townspeople, convinced that Constance has gotten away with murder and resentful of the Blackwoods' wealth, harass and tease Merricat whenever she leaves the estate to run errands. But Merricat is content with the new life she and Constance have created--until their estranged cousin Charles shows up and threatens to ruin it all. While the film lacks some of the subtlety of the book and changes a few plot points to make for a more dramatic story, it's still an overall faithful adaptation and has earned a place on this marathon.<br/>
<br/>
What movies are you planning to watch around Halloween this year? Have you seen any of the films discussed above? Share your thoughts in the comments!<br/>

<div class="subheading">
<a href="/journal_entry/8878615"><span class="subheading_text">5 Spooky Books Coming Out October 2019</span></a>
[Featured Site Content]
</div>
<div class="newsfeed_date">Oct 03, 2019, 07:12am
</div>
<a href="/VampireFreaks"><img alt="" class="newsfeed_entry_thumb" src="//pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/50845677_s.jpg"/></a>
<a class="newsfeed_entry_username" href="/VampireFreaks">VampireFreaks</a>
<div class="newsfeed_entry_txt message_limited">
<img class="entry_pic" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/contentpics.vampirefreaks.com/journal/8/88/887/8878615/8878615.jpg"/>
<div class="message_div_limited">
It's the most magical time of the year ... when all the best books come out! It's become a yearly tradition for me now to do a round-up of some of the new spooky books being released, so here are a few that I am most looking forward to: <br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>1. <i>The Vine Witch</i> by Ivanne G. Smith</b></font><br/>
<br/>
The month is already off to a great start with this debut novel, which came out on October 1. <i>The Vine Witch</i> is the first in a new series featuring witches in turn-of-the-century France. Elena was once a vine witch, tasked with using magic to care for the vineyards that produce the world-renowned wine of the Chanceaux Valley. But thanks to a curse, Elena has spent the last seven years transformed into a toad and stuck in the marshes. When she finally regains her human form, she tries to return to her old life only to find that her vineyards are now in the possession of a handsome stranger. Keeping her true identity hidden, Elena tries to nurse her beloved vineyard back to health while seeking revenge against whoever cursed her—and maybe also falling in love in the process. How better to start the Halloween season than with a fun witchy romance? <br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>2. <i>Imaginary Friend</i> by Stephen Chbosky </b></font> <br/>
<br/>
If you're looking for something a little darker, this heavy horror novel also came out at the beginning of the month. Stephen Chbosky is best known penning the quirky cult classic, <i>The Perks of Being a Wallflower</i> (1999). Now, two decades later, he's decided to shift gears completely for his second novel. Single mother Kate Reese just wants to start a quiet new life in a small town with her young son, Christopher. But after going missing for several terrifying days, seven-year-old Christopher emerges from the woods somewhat ... different. He now hears a voice in his head that demands he carry out a particular mission or else risk dire consequences for his family and the town. Reviewers have likened the story to both <i>Stranger Things</i> and the works of Stephen King. Beware, though: <i>Imaginary Friend</i> is a hefty tome at over 700 pages. So if you don't like your horror with a slow, slow, slooooow burn, this may not be for you.<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>3. <i>The Beautiful</i> by Renee Ahdieh </b></font> <br/>
<br/>
I stand by my belief that the vampire genre will never, ever die. This latest addition to that unending pool of teenage vampire romance comes out on October 8. The story is set in 19th-century New Orleans, where a serial killer is on the loose. When 17-year-old Celine Rousseau shows up in the city alone, she is taken in by the Ursuline convent and falls in love with the glitzy underworld of New Orleans. Among the city’s chief allures is the handsome Sèbastien Saint Germain. But when one of the other girls at the convent is found dead, Celine fears that the man she’s attracted to may be a killer. The bodies continue to pile up, and Celine is determined to uncover the murderer’s identity, no matter what it costs. <br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>4. <i>Ninth House</i> by Leigh Bardugo </b></font> <br/>
<br/>
Beloved YA author Leigh Bardugo (best known for <i>Six of Crows</i> and <i>Shadow and Bone</i>) is releasing her first book for adults on October 8. <i>Ninth House</i> explores the dark underbelly of one of the country's oldest institutions. After becoming the sole survivor of a gruesome, unsolved multiple homicide, Alex Stern is given a second chance at a better life with a full ride to Yale University. But there's a catch—she must spend her time there investigating the occult activities of Yale's secret societies. From ghosts to murder to the realities of trauma and abuse, this book will school you on just how dark the world can be.<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>5. <i>Laughter at the Academy</i> by Seanan McGuire </b></font> <br/>
<br/>
Seanan McGuire is one of my absolute favorite horror writers, but I usually have to seek out her short stories scattered across various anthologies. That's about to change! Coming out on October 31, <i>Laughter at the Academy</i> collects nearly two dozen of McGuire's short stories into one place for the first time. The collection includes tales of mad scientists, ghosts, creepy dolls, a Halloween that never ends, and much, much more!<br/>
<br/>
What are you planning to read this October? Are any of these on your list? Let me know in the comments!<br/>

<a href="/journal_entry/8878476"><span class="subheading_text">The Story Behind Frankenstein</span></a>
[Featured Site Content]
</div>
<div class="newsfeed_date">Sep 26, 2019, 06:48am
</div>
<a href="/VampireFreaks"><img alt="" class="newsfeed_entry_thumb" src="//pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/50845677_s.jpg"/></a>
<a class="newsfeed_entry_username" href="/VampireFreaks">VampireFreaks</a>
<div class="newsfeed_entry_txt message_limited">
<img class="entry_pic" src="//s3.amazonaws.com/contentpics.vampirefreaks.com/journal/8/88/887/8878476/8878476.jpg"/>
<div class="message_div_limited">
Most of us are familiar with the man-made monstrosity known as Frankenstein's Monster. Many of you may also be aware that before the monster became a grunting, green-skinned Hollywood sensation, he was the focus of a novel by a young woman named Mary Shelley. But who exactly was she? And how did she come to write a story so original that it transformed the horror genre and essentially invented science fiction? Well, it all started on a dark and stormy night....<br/>
<br/>
In the summer of 1816, 18-year old Mary Godwin, the daughter of a leading feminist and a prominent political philosopher, was vacationing in Europe with her lover—the poet Percy Shelley. The couple rented a house near Lake Geneva in Switzerland and made frequent visits to neighboring mansion, called Villa Diodati, where the somewhat disgraced public figure Lord Byron was staying. Byon's physician, John Polidori, and Mary's step-sister, Claire Clairmont, made up the rest of this merry band.<br/>
<br/>
Unfortunately, the weather did not cooperate with their vacation plans. The year 1816 was known as the "Year Without a Summer." About a year before, a volcano in Indonesia called Mt. Tambora had erupted, and clouds of ash blocked the sun, significantly altering the climate. The following year became one of the coldest years on record and that summer was particularly wet and rainy. <br/>
<br/>
With the party stuck indoors and running out of ways to entertain themselves, Lord Byron suggested that they hold a contest to see who could write the best ghost story. Around the same time, Mary had a nightmare in which she saw the image of a young scientist kneeling beside the creature he had just fashioned and brought to life. When she woke up, she knew what her scary story would be about. There in the villa, she read out a fragment of the story that would later evolve into the novel we know as <i>Frankenstein</i>, which she published a few years later in 1818. Despite being a young unknown in a house full of famous writers, Mary was the clear winner of the competition, as few of the others managed to produce anything that lasted.<br/>
<br/>
Byron wrote part of a vampire story, which he just called "Fragment of a Novel" and never finished. Polidori later used that fragment as the inspiration for his novella <i>The Vampyre</i> and modeled the blood-drinking Lord Ruthven on Lord Byron himself. Percy Shelley doesn't seem to have made any contribution to the contest. But think! If it weren't for a particularly rainy summer and a bored teenage girl, we might not have had the monster movies, the science fiction genre, and the rich tradition of horror literature that we know and love today.<br/>
<br/>
What do you think of Frankenstein's origin story? What other literary figures would you like to learn about? Let me know in the comments below!<br/>

<div class="subheading">
<a href="/journal_entry/8878263"><span class="subheading_text">Lovecraft's Literary Circle</span></a>
[Featured Site Content]
</div>
<div class="newsfeed_date">Sep 12, 2019, 06:54am
</div>
<a href="/VampireFreaks"><img alt="" class="newsfeed_entry_thumb" src="//pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/50845677_s.jpg"/></a>
<a class="newsfeed_entry_username" href="/VampireFreaks">VampireFreaks</a>
<div class="newsfeed_entry_txt message_limited">
<div class="message_div_limited">
People tend to imagine H. P. Lovecraft as a recluse, just sitting alone in a dark room dreaming up eldritch horrors. But old Howard actually had a pretty robust social life. He especially liked to keep up correspondence with fellow writers interested in the supernatural horror genre known as weird fiction. Lovecraft would forge connections with these writers and then introduce them to each other, creating a whole network of authors that have come to be referred to by critics as the "Lovecraft Circle." In their letters, Lovecraft and these other writers would often trade ideas, offer advice, and invite each other to write stories within their created worlds. So, if you're a fan of Lovecraft's writing, you may want to check out a few of these other authors from his literary circle:<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>Clark Ashton Smith</b></font><br/>
<br/>
<img alt="Clark Ashton Smith" src="http://pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/52161936.jpg?1568087272" style="width:50%;"/><br/>
<br/>
Smith was, at the time, one of the biggest writers of weird fiction. Like Lovecraft, he published many of his stories in the pulp magazine <i>Weird Tales</i>. Smith wrote quite a few stories set within or adjacent to Lovecraft's Cthulhu Mythos and even created his own eldritch gods to add to the pantheon. Smith introduced one of the Old Ones known as Tsathoggua in his story "The Tale of Satampra Zeiros," and Lovecraft later worked a reference to this being into one of his own stories, "The Whisperer in the Darkness." Likewise, Lovecraft makes many references to an occult tome known as the Book of Eibon, which is named for a wizard in Smith's story "The Door to Saturn." In turn, Smith references Lovecraft's Necronomicon in his story "The Return of the Sorcerer." Apart from playing in Lovecraft's playground, Smith also invented several fictional worlds of his own. Most of his works can be grouped into five story cycles based on their settings, which belong to one to one of the following worlds: Averoigne, a fantasy world resembling historic France; a prehistoric land called Hyperborea; Mars; Poseidonis, a remnant of the lost continent of Atlantis; and a futuristic continent called Zothique.<br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>Robert E. Howard</b></font><br/>
<br/>
<img alt="Robert E. Howard" src="http://pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/52161937.jpg?1568087341" style="width:50%;"/><br/>
<br/>
Along with Lovecraft and Smith, Robert E. Howard is the third member of the weird fiction trifecta that dominated <i>Weird Tales</i>. Howard was a decade and a half younger than Lovecraft. However, he started writing early and became a prominent contributor to the pulp magazines by the time he was 20. Howard wrote several stories set within the Cthulhu Mythos, including "The Black Stone," which introduces the mad poet Justin Geoffrey, whom Lovecraft later mentioned in "The Thing on the Doorstep." Another of Howard's Lovecraftian stories is "The Fire of Asshurbanipal," in which an American adventurer and his Afgan sidekick search the Arabian desert for an ancient City of Evil mentioned in the Necronomicon. But Howard also wrote pulp stories that were more action and adventure than cosmic horror. He is perhaps best known for creating the character Conan the Barbarian and is hailed as having invented the "sword and sorcery" subgenre of fantasy. He was also particularly fond of writing westerns. Howard died by suicide at the age of 30, which devastated Lovecraft and the other members of his circle. <br/>
<br/>
<font size="4px"><b>August Derleth</b></font><br/>
<br/>
<img alt="August Derleth" src="http://pics.vampirefreaks.com/V/Va/Vam/VampireFreaks/52161938.jpg?1568087379" style="width:50%;"/><br/>
<br/>
August Derleth can be credited with coining the phrase "Cthulhu Mythos" and was instrumental in building Lovecraft's inventions into a cohesive cosmology. Like Smith and Howard, Derleth was a pulp writer, publishing stories in the magazine Weird Tales. Derleth began corresponding with Lovecraft as a teenager, and over the years the two grew to be such close friends that Lovecraft appointed Derleth as his literary executor. After Lovecraft's death in 1937, Derleth took this duty seriously and founded a publishing company called Arkham House in order to collect and preserve his friend's best works. Through Arkham House, Derleth published a large anthology of Lovecraft's stories. He also took it upon himself to write new stories within the Cthulhu Mythos based on fragments and notes that Lovecraft had left behind. Several of these are collected in the volume The Survivor and Others, which gives "H. P. Lovecraft and August Derleth" as its byline, suggesting a posthumous collaboration. Some critics have objected to the ways in which Derleth essentially redefined Lovecraft's mythos and the fact that he used Lovecraft's name to sell what was essentially his own fiction, but it cannot be denied that Lovecraft wouldn't have quite the legacy he does today without the help of August Derleth. <br/>
<br/>
Have you read the works of any of the writers discussed above? What other literary circles would you like to learn about here? Let me know in the comments!<br/>
